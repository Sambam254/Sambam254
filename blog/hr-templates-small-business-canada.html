HR Tips for Small Business

5 Essential HR Templates Every Canadian Small Business Needs

By Samantha Russell  ·  November 2024  ·  8 min read

If you run a small business in Canada and you're still winging it when it comes to HR documentation, you're not alone — and you're also sitting on a liability that could cost you thousands.

Whether you have 2 employees or 20, having the right HR templates in place protects you legally, creates a professional experience for your team, and saves you hours every time you hire, review, or let someone go.

The good news: you don't need an HR department to have great HR documentation. You just need the right templates. Here are the five every Canadian small business owner should have on file.

Quick note on provincial law: Employment standards vary by province in Canada. The templates and guidance in this article are written primarily for Ontario employers (under the Employment Standards Act, 2000), but the core principles apply across most provinces. Always consult a legal or HR professional for your specific situation.

1. The Employment Offer Letter

An offer letter is often the first legal document your new hire receives from you. It sets the tone, establishes the terms of employment, and — critically — protects you if things go sideways later.

Without a signed offer letter, you have no written record of what was agreed to. That means disputes about salary, hours, or job duties have no paper trail. Courts have ruled against employers in situations where offer letters were verbal-only or vague.

What a strong offer letter should include:

  • Job title, department, and reporting structure
  • Start date, employment type (full-time, part-time, contract), and work location
  • Compensation (salary or hourly rate, pay frequency)
  • Benefits, vacation entitlement, and any applicable waiting periods
  • Probationary period length and terms
  • Conditions of employment (background check, signing NDA, etc.)
  • A signature line for the candidate to accept

2. The New Employee Onboarding Checklist

The first 90 days of employment are the highest-risk period for turnover. Research consistently shows that employees who go through a structured onboarding process are significantly more likely to stay past the one-year mark.

An onboarding checklist isn't just good culture — it's good business. It ensures nothing gets missed, creates consistency across hires, and gives your new employee a clear sense of what to expect.

A solid onboarding checklist covers:

  • Before Day 1: paperwork, payroll setup, system access, team notification
  • Day 1: workspace setup, health and safety orientation, team introductions
  • Week 1: role training, tools and systems, communication norms
  • 30/60/90-day check-ins with documented notes

Ontario note: Under the Occupational Health and Safety Act, employers are required to provide health and safety orientation to new workers. A formal onboarding checklist helps you document that this was completed.

3. The Performance Review Template

Performance reviews aren't just about annual raises. Done right, they are one of the most powerful tools a small business owner has for retaining talent, addressing issues early, and creating a culture of accountability and growth.

Many small businesses skip formal reviews entirely — and then are shocked when an employee claims they were never told about performance issues before termination. That's a legal and HR nightmare that a simple documented review can prevent.

An effective performance review template includes:

  • A clear, consistent rating scale (e.g., 1-5)
  • Core competency categories (quality of work, communication, teamwork, etc.)
  • Space to review previous goals and set new ones
  • A development/training section
  • Employee self-assessment section
  • Signature lines for both manager and employee

4. The Employee Warning Letter

If you need to discipline an employee, documentation is everything. Progressive discipline — verbal warning, written warning, final warning, termination — is the standard in Canadian employment law, and it requires a paper trail.

Employers who skip documentation and jump straight to termination are routinely ordered to pay damages in wrongful dismissal claims. A simple, well-written warning letter that is delivered, discussed, and signed by both parties gives you significant legal protection.

A warning letter should document:

  • The specific issue or behaviour (dates, facts, impact)
  • Prior warnings or conversations already had
  • Clear, measurable expectations going forward
  • What support the employer will provide
  • Consequences if the issue continues
  • Signature lines and an option for employee response

5. The Termination Letter and Notice Period Guide

Letting someone go is one of the most stressful things a small business owner faces. The stakes are high — both legally and humanly. A proper termination letter protects you legally and treats the employee with dignity.

In Ontario, the Employment Standards Act sets minimum notice requirements based on years of service. Missing these requirements — even by accident — can expose you to a Ministry of Labour complaint or a civil claim.

What every termination letter must address:

  • The effective date of termination
  • Whether the employee is working through notice or receiving pay in lieu
  • The amount of notice or severance owed (per ESA minimums)
  • Final pay details including accrued vacation
  • Benefits continuation or cutoff date
  • Return of company property
  • Reminder of any ongoing confidentiality obligations

Ontario ESA Notice Minimums: Less than 1 year = 1 week. 1-3 years = 2 weeks. 3-8 years = 3-7 weeks (1 per year). 8+ years = 8 weeks. These are minimums — common law "reasonable notice" is often much higher for longer-service employees.
